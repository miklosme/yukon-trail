# The Yukon Trail: a journey from Seattle to the Klondike

A detailed, human-readable reconstruction of the completed Linda–Chilkoot run.
Prepared October 12, 2026.

## What this account covers

This is the story of one observed journey through the original Windows game,
from its opening in Seattle to its ending newspaper and final score. It follows
the player and Linda aboard the Yosemite, through Dyea and the Chilkoot Pass,
across the winter to Lake Bennett, downriver to Dawson, and into the goldfields
at Bonanza Creek.

The account is based on the [recorded gameplay chat](codex://threads/01a10a23-49f3-75c0-8411-72601d0b2483),
the [contemporaneous playthrough report](BLIND-PLAYTHROUGH.md), and preserved
original-game screenshots. It is a narrative paraphrase, not a complete dialogue
transcript. The player used the name `blind`; below, “you” means that player.

The events and choices described here were observed in that run. Connecting
comments about the journey's meaning are interpretation. Other partners,
routes, choices, and outcomes were not explored, so this is a complete account
of the recorded path rather than an inventory of every story the game can tell.

## 1. Seattle: the expedition begins

Your journey begins in Seattle on August 23, 1897. You have $350 and no food.
The goal is to reach the northern goldfields and try your luck as a prospector,
but first you need transport, equipment, provisions, and someone to travel with.

From the wharf, you visit downtown Seattle and Pioneer Outfitters. The merchant
will sell an outfit for two travelers and directs you to the ticket office to
find a partner. Preparing for the expedition starts with forming a partnership.

At the ticket office you meet Linda. She says she was born and raised in the
destination area and can help lead you to gold. You agree to travel together.
The journal records the partnership on August 24, describing Linda as coming
from Alaska and bringing knowledge of the area and packing experience. She
also contributes $500 to your shared funds.

You buy two tickets to Dyea aboard the Yosemite for $60. You then return to
the outfitter and purchase a $370 outfit with 1,600 pounds of food. The merchant
describes cooking equipment, mining equipment, cold-weather clothing, and a
sled as part of the outfit. You also ask about a whipsaw: a two-person saw used
to cut boards. It costs another $8, and you buy it.

Those preparations leave the partnership with $412. The saw will become
relevant much later, when reaching the next stage of the route requires
building a boat.

At the wharf, you confirm that you have tickets and are ready. The Yosemite
has not yet returned, so you must wait about a week. The calendar advances,
and the journal records boarding on August 31. The expedition finally leaves
Seattle.

## 2. The Yosemite: a difficult voyage north

News aboard ship warns that both the Chilkoot and White Pass trails are crowded.
Even before reaching land, you learn that many other travelers are trying to
make the same journey.

The voyage becomes dangerous. Linda reports a bad storm and worries about
whether the ship can withstand it. The journal records a severe storm on
September 6 and possible damage to cargo. On September 15, the ship strikes
rocks; the observed conversation describes a little water coming aboard and
possible cargo damage. A further journal entry records an iceberg strike on
September 21, also damaging cargo.

You acknowledge the reports and continue. Trying the travel-speed control
produces an explanation that the ship has already been chosen and its speed
cannot now be changed. At this stage, the consequences of your earlier choice
must play out.

The voyage also includes an educational scene about totem poles. Such scenes
continue throughout the journey, interrupting travel with information about
places and features along the route. The surviving report identifies these
subjects but does not preserve their full text.

The Yosemite reaches Dyea on September 27. The following day's journal shows
1,309 pounds of food and $412. You have survived the sea passage, but the
overland journey has yet to begin.

## 3. Dyea to Sheep Camp: the crowded Chilkoot Trail

In Dyea you choose the Chilkoot route and set out on September 28. The journey
now proceeds on foot through a landscape of difficult trails, crowds, and
worsening weather.

On October 3, a thief takes 112 pounds of food. There is no recovery choice in
the observed encounter; you acknowledge the loss and keep moving. An
educational scene introduces the Corduroy Road.

Progress repeatedly stops. On October 6, the trail is so crowded that nobody
can move. A rock slide causes another delay on October 12. The next day,
a blinding blizzard forces you to remain in your tent until the snow stops.

You reach Sheep Camp on October 27. The October 28 journal lists 993 pounds
of food and the same $412. Nearly a month has passed since leaving Dyea,
and the provisions have fallen substantially. You continue from camp without
buying more food or hiring a dog team.

The journal contains an inconsistency here: it also records a ship hitting an
iceberg on October 3 while labeling the location as the Chilkoot Trail. That
entry is not treated as another literal sea voyage in this reconstruction.
The earlier September 21 iceberg entry belongs to the Yosemite voyage;
the cause of the later misplaced entry is unknown.

## 4. The pass and customs: the expedition is turned back

Soon after leaving Sheep Camp, Linda catches her foot between rocks and sprains
her ankle. The journal dates the injury to October 31. You choose to stop and
rest instead of pressing on immediately.

The ascent continues through the Summit–Scales area, with educational scenes
about the Scales and the Golden Stairs. A horse falls and blocks the trail on
November 5, causing another delay. You reach Canadian Customs on November 10.

There, an officer welcomes you to Canada, but your request to pass is refused.
Your party needs a total of 2,000 pounds of food and $100 for the tax. You do
not have enough food. The officer says he will guard the things you have
brought, but you must return for more supplies. The journal records the
turnback on November 11.

This is the largest reversal of the recorded journey. After crossing the sea
and spending weeks climbing toward the pass, you have reached a checkpoint
that preparation and supplies determine whether you can cross. Your remaining
money is sufficient for the tax, but money alone does not satisfy the food
requirement.

You head back down toward Sheep Camp. Crowds and snow continue to slow the
journey. On November 20 you fall and hurt yourself; again you choose to rest.
By December 2 you are back at Sheep Camp, with 861 pounds of food and $412.

The outfitter explains that food comes in 400-pound packages costing $60 each.
He describes consumption of about 100 pounds per person per month and the
customs requirement of 1,000 pounds per person. You buy another 1,600 pounds
for $240.

You climb toward the border a second time, passing the Scales and Golden
Stairs again. This time the officer accepts your supplies and asks for the
$100 tax. You pay and are allowed onward. After the food purchase and tax,
the partnership has $72 left.

## 5. Across the winter to Lake Bennett

Passing customs does not end the difficult travel. The onward route is now a
winter journey, with repeated blizzards and a mule-train delay. The calendar
advances through December and into 1898.

You sprain your ankle on January 3 and choose another rest. A Lake Lindeman
educational scene appears as the journey continues toward Lake Bennett.

You finally arrive at Lake Bennett on January 27. The next day's journal
shows 2,077 pounds of food and $72. You have successfully brought the expedition
beyond the pass, but the water route cannot yet be used.

At the lake, you are told to wait for spring and build a boat. You ask how to
do this and learn that logs, a whipsaw, and the posted boat plans are what you
need. When you ask where to obtain logs, you are directed to abandoned logs
beside the lake.

You consult the Boat Plans sign, select the first illustrated plan, and choose
to build. The run did not compare the plans or establish their individual
strengths. It simply followed Plan 1.

The calendar moves forward. When spring comes and the ice breaks, an officer
says your boat has been examined and is fit to travel. Because of your late
arrival, you must wait four more days before departing. You accept the wait.
The journal records the first boat trip on June 2, 1898.

## 6. Downriver: three dangerous passages

The expedition now changes from overland travel to a journey by boat.
Educational scenes introduce Caribou Crossing and the Fifty Mile River.
Then the river presents hazards you must actively navigate.

At Miles Canyon, a location image leads into a rafting sequence. The game's
Help explains that moving the pointer over the water steers the boat and that
boat type affects maneuverability. You try to guide the vessel through clear
water, but it is damaged.

You are offered a choice about how to continue. Repairing is described as
taking two days, compared with a week to build a new boat. You choose to try
repairing it and resume the journey.

White Horse Rapids brings a second rafting sequence. The boat is damaged
again, and again you choose repairs. Travel continues in June.

At Five Finger Rapids, you face a third course. You steer through the visible
water, suffer more boat damage, and choose repairs for the third time.
The river map subsequently shows July 2.

The evidence establishes damage and recovery at each of the three passages.
It does not establish exactly which rocks or collisions caused each outcome.
For the story, the important consequence is clear: the boat is repeatedly
damaged, but you keep repairing it and pressing toward Dawson.

## 7. Dawson: reaching the destination is only the beginning

You arrive in Dawson City on July 6, 1898, more than ten months after the
opening in Seattle. The July 7 journal lists 1,837 pounds of food and $72.

Arrival does not end the game. It puts you in a position to attempt the
purpose of the expedition: finding and mining gold.

You use Dawson's Claims board to open a claim map and select an available
marker near the Bonanza Creek label. The site is identified as No. 5 Below
Bonanza Creek.

Linda tells you to choose a spot and dig. Your first test beside the creek
yields gold dust. You decide to test the claim further and select another
spot farther upriver. That test leaves a plain mound and produces no new
dialogue in the observed sequence.

When you use the Trail sign to leave, the game asks whether you want to stake
the claim. You decide to do so. That choice commits the expedition to working
the claim you have tested.

## 8. Mining the claim

Staking the claim leads into an automatic mining sequence. Miners work,
the gold counter increases, and calendar pages turn. The recorded run does
not show a separate chain of management decisions during this sequence.

The counter reaches 14,514. Eventually a notice says winter is coming and
directs you to cash in and head home using the Trail sign. November 2, 1898
is displayed before the ending.

You acknowledge the notice and leave the claim. The expedition has made it
through preparation, sea travel, the mountain trail, a forced retreat, winter,
the river, and a mining season. The remaining question is how the game judges
what you have achieved.

## 9. The ending: enough to get home

The closing conversation congratulates you for reaching Dawson and points
out that many stampeders never made it that far. It adds that a little more
luck might have allowed you to strike it rich.

An ending newspaper then appears. Its headline is “Klondike Pair Reaches
Dawson,” and its subheadline is “They Find Enough Gold to Pay for Passage Home.”

That is the outcome of this particular story. You and Linda have survived
the expedition, reached the goldfields, and found gold. The newspaper frames
the result as enough to return home, rather than a great fortune. The closing
dialogue gives reaching Dawson some value in itself, while leaving the larger
dream of striking it rich unrealized.

The final Claims of Fame screen lists the player as a **Stampeder** with a
score of **14,586**, dated **July 6, 1898**. That date matches arrival in Dawson,
not the November date shown before the ending. The displayed score equals
the observed mining counter of 14,514 plus the $72 held at Dawson, but that
arithmetic is only a plausible explanation of this result; the general score
formula has not been established.

Selecting Done returns the game to Seattle Wharf and a fresh name-entry
prompt. There is no observed playable return voyage. Homecoming is conveyed
through the closing conversation and newspaper, followed by the score and
the option to begin another expedition.

## Reading the story as a whole

The following is interpretation of the recorded events. The journey's arc is
one of ambition repeatedly meeting practical constraints. At Seattle, the
expedition seems to be a matter of choosing a partner and buying supplies.
The stormy voyage and crowded trail reveal how much remains outside your
control. Customs turns preparation into a decisive obstacle: you must go
back, spend more money, and repeat the climb. Lake Bennett requires patience
and construction. The river requires navigation and repairs. Dawson finally
lets you prospect, but reaching the goldfields does not guarantee wealth.

Linda accompanies the player through this arc as a partner, financial
contributor, source of local knowledge, and participant in its hardships.
The observed run does not establish a separate personal quest or later
resolution for her beyond the pair's expedition outcome.

The resulting story is a completed gold-rush expedition with a modest ending:
you get there, find some gold, and have enough to get home. Its specific
setbacks and ending belong to this run; they should not be mistaken for
mandatory events in every playthrough.
